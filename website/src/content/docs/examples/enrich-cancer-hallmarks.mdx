Cancer is not one pathway. Every tumour type runs on its own mix of proliferation, survival, and invasion
programmes, and the genes behind those programmes are shared across the diseases. If that shared machinery is
really written in the gene sets, then a method that knows nothing but which pathways happen to be cancers should
be able to point back at the machinery they have in common.

`enrich` tests exactly that. Given a reference module, a set of groups you already care about, it walks the
similarity graph and asks, for every other group, whether that group's neighbours are unexpectedly full of module
members. A pathway surrounded by cancer pathways in gene space is scored with a hypergeometric test and ranked.
The groups it surfaces are guilt-by-association candidates: tied to the module by the company they keep, without
being in it.

The panel is the 186 KEGG pathways (Kanehisa and Goto, 2000) as distributed by MSigDB (Liberzon et al., 2011),
covering the genome-wide pathway map.

## The analysis, step by step

**Index the pathways.** `index` reads the GMT, one pathway and its gene symbols per line, and builds a hashed
feature index (`kegg.dbri`) so pathways can be compared by shared genes without re-parsing gene lists.

```bash
DBRetina index -g kegg.gmt -o kegg
```

**Compare every pair.** `pairwise` scores all 17,205 pathway pairs into the directory `kegg_DBRetina_pairwise/`.
`-m ochiai` is the Ochiai coefficient, the shared genes divided by the geometric mean of the two set sizes, a
size-aware overlap that does not inflate for large pathways. `-c 0` keeps every pair, so the directory holds the
full similarity graph.

```bash
DBRetina pairwise -i kegg -m ochiai -c 0
```

**Define the module.** The module is just a text file of pathway names, one per line. We take every pathway whose
name mentions a cancer, straight from the GMT you already have:

```bash
grep -iE 'cancer|carcinoma|leukemia|melanoma|glioma' kegg.gmt | cut -f1 > cancer_module.txt
```

That is 15 pathways (breast, prostate, glioma, melanoma, the leukaemias, and so on), defined from names alone. No
gene information goes into the definition, so recovering it from gene overlap is a fair test, not a circular one.

**Rank by guilt-by-association.** `enrich` takes the module and, for each pathway, counts how many of its
neighbours are cancer pathways, where a neighbour is any pathway joined by an edge of at least 20% Ochiai
(`-m ochiai -c 20`). It scores that count with a hypergeometric test: given that 15 of the 186 pathways are
cancer, how surprising is it that this pathway has, say, 12 of its 26 neighbours in the module? `--exclude-module`
drops the cancer pathways from the output, leaving only the candidates.

```bash
DBRetina enrich -d kegg_DBRetina_pairwise --module cancer_module.txt -m ochiai -c 20 --exclude-module
```

It prints the ranked candidates, strongest first (names shortened here):

```text

focal_adhesion            12    26         1.4e-09
b_cell_receptor_signaling 12    30         1.17e-08
erbb_signaling            12    31         1.87e-08

```

Every leading hit is a Hallmark of Cancer (Hanahan and Weinberg, 2011). ErbB (EGFR and HER2), mTOR, insulin, and
neurotrophin signaling are the proliferative-signaling programmes, and EGFR and HER2 are among the most validated
drug targets in oncology. Apoptosis is resisting cell death, VEGF signaling is inducing angiogenesis, and focal
adhesion and actin-cytoskeleton regulation are activating invasion and metastasis. Ranked and scored, with the
evidence on each bar, the same hits read straight off the list of Hallmarks:

![Each flagged pathway states its evidence: how many of its gene-sharing neighbours are cancer pathways, coloured by the Hallmark of Cancer it embodies](/DBRetina/img/enrich_cancer_hallmarks.png)

The B-cell and T-cell receptor pathways surface for a real reason too: they run on the same PI3K, MAPK, and NF-kB
kinases as the oncogenic module, and B-cell receptor signaling is itself a driver of B-cell lymphoma and the
target of BTK inhibitors such as ibrutinib (Young and Staudt, 2013). Told nothing but a list of cancer names,
`enrich` reconstructs the core of cancer biology.

## Is the recovery real?

Press **Hold out a cancer pathway** on the graph above. Each time, one cancer pathway is hidden from the module,
yet most of its gene-sharing neighbours are still cancer pathways, so `enrich` ranks it straight back near the top.
Doing that for all fifteen in turn (leave-one-out), the enrichment score separates the cancer pathways from the
rest with an AUC of 0.986. Against a null of 2000 random modules of the same size, drawn from the same pathways,
the mean recovery AUC is 0.48, and not one random module reached the real value: 4.9 standard deviations above the
null, permutation p below 0.0005. The signal is specific to the biology, not the method. A random set of 15
pathways does not recover itself, because random pathways do not share genes, while the cancer pathways do.

## When to use this in practice

The cancer run above is a *validation*, not a use case. We seeded `enrich` with a set we already knew, the cancer
pathways, only to prove the ranking is trustworthy: hide part of a known answer, and it comes back. In a real
analysis you run it the other way round. You give `enrich` a **partial set you are confident about**, and the top
of the ranking is the part you do **not** know yet: candidates, each with a p-value, to follow up.

- **Extend a candidate list.** You have a handful of genes confirmed in a disease. `enrich` ranks the rest of the
  genome by how much each one's neighbourhood looks like your seed, and the top hits are the genes most worth
  testing next.
- **Grow a module from an experiment.** A screen or an RNA-seq run flags a few pathways. `enrich` returns the
  pathways most likely co-involved but not yet on your list.
- **Repurpose a drug.** Seed with the drugs known to work for a condition; `enrich` surfaces others whose target
  neighbourhoods match, as repurposing leads.
- **Label an unknown group.** Run `enrich` on an unannotated cluster against a known module to see what it most
  resembles.

`enrich` is a hypothesis generator: it turns "I know a few X" into "here are the next most likely X, ranked and
scored." It prioritises what to look at next, it does not settle it. The exact test is in the
[enrich reference](/DBRetina/commands/enrich/#how-it-works).

## Reproduce it yourself

Everything runs from
[`usecases/enrich-cancer-hallmarks/`](https://github.com/DBRetina/DBRetina/tree/main/usecases/enrich-cancer-hallmarks)
in the repository (the `run.sh`, the analysis scripts, and the data all live there).

1. **Install DBRetina** (the [quickstart](/DBRetina/start/quickstart/)) and the Python packages the validation and
   figures use: `scipy`, `pyarrow`, `matplotlib`, `networkx`.
2. **Get the gene sets.** They are the KEGG subset of MSigDB's canonical pathways,
   `c2.cp.kegg.v2023.1.Hs.symbols.gmt`, from the [MSigDB downloads](https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/),
   saved as `data/kegg.gmt`.
3. **Run** `bash run.sh`. It indexes the GMT, builds the pairwise, writes the module, runs `enrich`, validates
   (leave-one-out plus the random-module null), and regenerates both figures and the network above. The
   validation prints:

```text
KEGG pathways: 186 | edges at ochiai>=20% | active groups: 154
cancer module (by name): 15 pathways

HELD-OUT RECOVERY (leave-one-out)
  real AUC          = 0.986
  null AUC (2000x)  = 0.479 +/- 0.103   (95th pct 0.649)
  4.9 SD above null   permutation p = 5.00e-04   (0 of 2000 random modules reached it)
```
